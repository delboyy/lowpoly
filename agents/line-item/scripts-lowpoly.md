# Next low-poly money stories: scripts (2026-10-27)

Formula from the penny video (Nathan: "the script is actually engaging and hooks the user"):
**Hook** (a shocking true line, in the first second) → **set-up** (how it worked) → **"so what happened?"** re-hook → **twist** → **payoff** that loops back to the hook. 30–40 s, max 4 figures, calm voice.

---

## 1. "Two pizzas. Eight hundred million dollars." (Bitcoin pizza)
- **Hook bar:** He paid $800 million for two pizzas
- **Script:**
  - In 2010, a programmer in Florida got hungry.
  - He offered ten thousand bitcoin to anyone who'd send him two pizzas.
  - Someone took the deal. Back then, those coins were worth about forty dollars.
  - [lowered] So why does this story still hurt?
  - Today, those ten thousand bitcoin are worth almost eight hundred million dollars.
  - And every May the twenty-second, people celebrate it. It's called Bitcoin Pizza Day.
  - [quiet] The most expensive dinner in history. And he says he doesn't regret it.
- **Figures:** 10,000 BTC · ~$40 · ~$800M · May 22 (date)
- **Visuals:** a low-poly laptop, coins flowing into a pizza box, two pizzas, a coin pile growing to a mountain, a calendar flip to "May 22", the pizza box closing (loop).
- **Facts to lock:**
  - Laszlo Hanyecz, 22 May 2010, 10,000 BTC for two pizzas (well documented).
  - ~$40 is the commonly cited value at the time; I still need a primary source.
  - **$800M** = 10,000 × $79,935, BTC spot on 27 Oct 2026 from CoinGecko/Coinbase. Re-check on posting day, because it moves.
  - "Doesn't regret it": he has said so in interviews; I need to source a quote.

## 2. "Coke cost a nickel for 73 years." (Coca-Cola nickel)
- **Hook bar:** Coke cost 5¢ for 73 years. Here's why.
- **Script:**
  - From 1886 to 1959, a bottle of Coke cost five cents. Wars, depressions, inflation: still a nickel.
  - Why? Thousands of vending machines only took one coin. A nickel.
  - [lowered] So when costs went up, what could Coke do?
  - It asked the US government for a new coin: seven and a half cents.
  - The government said no.
  - [quiet] So for seventy-three years, the price of Coke was set by the size of a coin.
- **Figures:** 5¢ · 73 years · 7.5¢
- **Visuals:** a low-poly glass bottle on a rising timeline (1886 to 1959), a vending machine with a nickel slot, a giant 7½¢ coin being stamped "REJECTED", the bottle returning to frame (loop).
- **Facts:**
  - Price fixed 1886–1959; vending machines and the 7.5¢ coin request come from Levy & Young (2003), "The Real Thing: Nominal Price Rigidity of the Nickel Coke, 1886–1959", and NPR Planet Money (2012).
  - The "73 years" in the script and hook bar are my arithmetic, 1959 − 1886. NPR rounds to "70 years".
  - Still to source: who asked, and the exact wording.

## 3. "He sold the Eiffel Tower. Then tried again."
- **Hook bar:** The man who sold the Eiffel Tower
- **Script:**
  - In 1925, a con man sold the Eiffel Tower.
  - He posed as a government official and told scrap dealers that Paris was tearing it down.
  - One dealer paid him, and he fled. The dealer was too embarrassed to report it.
  - [lowered] So what did he do next?
  - He came back and tried to sell it again.
  - [quiet] This time, the buyer went to the police. The tower is still standing.
- **Figures:** 1925 only (it's a story, not stats).
- **Visuals:** a low-poly Eiffel Tower, a briefcase of cash, a train leaving, the same tower again with a police light, and an ending wide shot of the tower (loop).
- **Facts to lock:** Victor Lustig and the 1925 sale, the dealer's name and the second attempt. These are widely told, but not verified yet; I need a solid source (Secret Service history, a book) before building.
